Bachelor’s Thesis (2026)

Single-Image 3D Shape Reconstruction Using Deep Learning

Turning one image of a sofa into a 3D model. Built in Python and PyTorch, with three approaches compared on 100 unseen sofas.

  • Deep learning
  • 3D reconstruction
  • Computer graphics
Overview Dataset Architecture Implementation Results Limitations Thesis & slides

Overview

The goal is to turn a single 2D image into a complete 3D model, without a 3D scanner, multiple photographs or manual modeling. One image cannot reveal the whole object, so the model has to infer hidden surfaces from the shapes it learned during training.

For my bachelor’s thesis, I built and compared three deep learning pipelines, from preparing the 3D data and rendering images to training models and evaluating the reconstructed meshes.

The research question

Is it better to learn the shape directly from an RGB image, or first extract information about its geometry? I compared RGB input with predicted surface normals, and with predicted normals combined with depth.

The project was completed at the School of Electrical Engineering, University of Belgrade, under the supervision of Prof. Dr. Goran Kvaščev, in July 2026.

Dataset & preparation

I limited the experiment to sofas from ModelNet40, using synthetic images of untextured models to focus on geometry. Of the 780 models, 680 were used for training and validation, and 100 were held out for testing. Splits are separated by object, so views of the same sofa never appear in different splits.

  • Mesh preparation. Center and scale the models, correct incorrectly oriented normals, and simplify the geometry.
  • RGB rendering. Generate 256 × 256 images from 24 camera positions across a 120° frontal range. Lighting and appearance are controlled to focus the experiment on geometry.
  • Geometry supervision. Use ray casting to generate reference normal and depth maps, with object masks separating foreground from background.
  • Shape supervision. Sample 100,000 signed-distance points per model: 80% near the surface and 20% throughout the surrounding volume.

Architecture

All three pipelines use a DeepSDF-style decoder to represent the 3D shape. What changes is the input to the encoder: the original image, predicted normals, or predicted normals and depth. Each encoder converts its input into a latent vector, a compact numerical description of the shape.

Predicting visible geometry

Two U-Nets predict the visible geometry: one estimates which way each surface faces (surface normals), and the other estimates its distance from the camera (depth). Both have four encoder–decoder levels, skip connections and group normalization. Training measures errors only on the sofa, excluding the background.

The normal model learns from direction errors and mean squared error (MSE). The depth model combines absolute error (L1) and MSE. Brightness and gamma changes vary the training images while keeping the target geometry unchanged.

Learning a 3D shape space

DeepSDF represents a shape through a signed distance function (SDF). For any point in 3D space, this function gives its distance from the object’s surface: positive outside, negative inside and zero on the surface. An eight-layer neural network predicts this value from the point’s coordinates and a 256-number latent vector.

The decoder and one latent vector per training sofa are learned together from sampled points and their reference distances. Once trained, the decoder can reconstruct a surface from its latent vector.

From an image to a mesh

Three ResNet34 encoders, initialized with ImageNet weights, learn to predict this latent vector from their respective inputs. The trained decoder stays fixed. The normals-and-depth variant takes four channels: three for surface direction and one for depth.

To produce the final model, the decoder predicts surface distances on a 128 × 128 × 128 grid. Marching Cubes turns those predictions into a mesh. A new image needs no extra optimization of its shape vector.

Training & implementation

The system is implemented in Python and PyTorch. Geometry processing uses trimesh and point-cloud-utils. Pyrender generates RGB views, embreex accelerates ray casting, and scikit-image extracts meshes. SciPy is used for nearest-neighbor distances during evaluation.

Training is staged: first the two U-Nets, then the shape auto-decoder, and finally the three image encoders. The encoders share the same decoder, split and training protocol so their input representations can be compared consistently.

Geometry prediction
80 epochs for each U-Net, with AdamW and cosine learning-rate decay.
Shape representation
2,000 epochs for the auto-decoder and its per-shape latent vectors.
Image encoders
200 epochs per input variant, batch size 16, with views sampled dynamically during training.

Results & interpretation

The predicted normal and depth maps capture the visible geometry well, with most errors around edges and abrupt surface changes. On the test set, the mean normal error is 4.63° and the mean absolute depth error is 0.0196, with depth values normalized to the range 0–1.

The final meshes are evaluated on 100 unseen sofas using Chamfer distance against the reference geometry. Smaller distances indicate a closer match.

Direct RGB gave the best final reconstruction.

The geometry-based encoders had a small advantage when given reference maps. Once the full pipelines used predicted maps, direct RGB achieved the lowest mean Chamfer distance, 0.0310. The differences were small, but the extra geometry prediction stages introduced errors that carried through to the final mesh.

Limitations & next steps

The unsuccessful reconstructions fall into two main groups: unusual shapes and missing small details. These results are limited to synthetic sofa images, and have not been established for real photographs or other object categories.

  • Unusual shapes drift towards common ones. Better representation of rare geometries, weighted sampling and a larger latent space are possible improvements.
  • Small details disappear. Thin legs and narrow supports contribute little to the overall loss. Targeted near-surface sampling and a higher extraction resolution could help preserve them.

The next step is to investigate these specific failures and test targeted improvements to sampling, shape representation and mesh resolution. I would address them before adding more geometric inputs, materials, textures or object categories.
